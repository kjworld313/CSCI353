### SUMMARY
This report details a method for sorting the MSCS Department’s showcase dinner line. The method utilizes a “four-corners” technique to account for each person in each major, marks their position in line as a valid position (the total number of people in the line is the total number of valid positions), and sorts each person into their major’s section of the line. The algorithm is called “Group Sort” because the algorithm removes the individuality of each person from consideration and “groups” them into their categories to pull from for each position in the line. ### APPROACH
Modeling the problem: Our strategy will eventually be written into an algorithm, so we must consider how to model the problem. Let us consider how to represent the people in the line and the line itself:
We only organize the line in terms of three possible college majors within St. Olaf’s MSCS department and people who have not yet declared their major (undeclared). Each major will be represented as a single-letter string: (“u”: undeclared, “c”: computer science”, “m”: mathematics, “s”: statistics and data science). If an individual’s major is unaccounted for, they will be designated as “u” or undeclared (if we do so, they will be one of the first in line, so we may be able to recruit them from their current department using fresh food!). 
Similarly, each person in the line is a representative of their major. Therefore, we will also represent them as a single-character string. If their major is mathematics, their major is modeled as “m,” and they themselves are also modeled as “m” in the line. This approach will allow us to remove individuality from each person in the line, as we are only considering their major for sorting the line. 
The line itself will be an array consisting of strings, where each string represents a person representing their major (ex: if our line has two people, “u” and “m”, our line would look like the following in code: [“u”, “m”]). The line is in the correct order when all of the values in the dictionary containing the number of each major in the line are 0.
*General idea:*
First, I will detail the additional data structures I will use. There are four college majors defining the order of the line: undeclared, computer science, mathematics, and statistics and data science. Therefore, I will use a map or dictionary called “groups” to inform the sort, where each key is a major and its value is the total number of people representing each major in the line (ex: if we have two undeclared majors, no computer science majors, one mathematics major, and four statistics and data science majors, our dictionary will look like the following: {“u”: 2, “c”: 0, “m”: 1, “s”: 4}). I will also create a second dictionary with each major as a key, but with the starting and ending positions of each major’s section in the line as values (for the example above (first position starts at 0): {“u”: (0,1), “c”: (-1, -1), “m”: (2,2), “s”: (3,6)}). Undeclared majors are first in line, so position 0 and the position prior to the starting position of the next major defines the undeclared major section of the line and so on. In the event that one or multiple majors lack representatives in the line, the starting and ending indices will be reflected as (-1, -1) to indicate invalid positions. 
Once I establish the total number of people in each major of “groups” (found by checking each person in the line and accounting for them in groups), mark all positions in the line as an open spot with “.”, and calculate the starting and ending positions of each major’s section in the line, I will begin sorting the line. If the position is valid for a person from one of the majors, I will assign them the position in line and account for their movement to the position in groups by decrementing the total number of people for their major. After the last person stands at a valid position in their major’s section in line, all values in groups representing the total number of each group to sort are 0, and the line is in the correct order. 
**Pseudocode:**
function group_sort:
	input: line (array of strings), size (int)
	output: none
	
	if line is empty or has one person:
		return
	groups <- {"u": 0, "c": 0, "m": 0, "s": 0}
	
	for person in line:
		if person's major is not in groups:
			assign person as undeclared
		groups[person] <- groups[person] + 1
		assign person's position in line as "."
	
	calculate starting and ending indices of each major's section
	
	valid_pos <- dictionary of each major (keys) and a tuple indicating 
    starting and ending index of their section in line (values)	

    for int p = 0 through size - 1:
        if p in range of undeclared section and groups["u"] > 0:
            line[p] <- "u"
            decrement number of undeclared majors in groups
        else if p in range of compsci section and groups["c"] > 0:
            line[p] <- "c"
            decrement number of computer science majors in groups
        else if p in range of math section and groups["m"] > 0:
            line[p] <- "m"
            decrement number of mathematics majors in groups
        else:
            line[p] <- "s"
            decrement number of stats and data science majors in groups

### ANALYSIS
*Completeness:* The approach will always result in students ending up in the correct order. Rather than swapping people, I utilize a strategy similar to the common “four-corners” method often used in game rooms or schools. I designate each position in line as empty and pull a person from each “corner” (or major in groups) to place in the position if there are still people remaining in the major to sort. The starting and ending positions are always calculated based on the previous section’s indices, so varying starting and ending positions are always accounted for (for example, if some majors have no representatives or the undeclared major specifically has no representatives being at the front of the line). Once the algorithm is complete, there are no more people to sort, each “corner” is empty, and the line is in order.
*Runtime complexity:* Θ(n) time complexity for program runtime.

    1 step -> O(1)

First, let us consider the base case: checking whether the line is empty or has one person and ending the function if the line meets the base case requirements. I will designate this as one step (for simplicity sake (i)) which occurs once in the function’s overall runtime (number of occurrences is independent of the size of the line array), so the base case check takes constant time. Next, we consider the time it takes to create our groups dictionary. Since our dictionary or map is initialized and declared with four key-value pairs, independent of the line’s size, we consider this as one step as well (ii), thus taking constant time. 
Now, we must count the total occurrence of each major in the line. The loop conditional occurs a total of n (size) times throughout the program, as we must perform the check on each position in the line which is equivalent to the total number of people in the line or size. Within each iteration, we have about 3 steps: handling a non-established category major, incrementing the number of people in the major represented by each person in a position or iteration, and assigning each person’s position in line as “.”. These three steps occur once per iteration, but n times throughout the entire program runtime because they are performed a number of times equivalent to the total number of iterations of the loop. Thus, the time complexity of the three steps within the loop is O(1), but the entire loop takes 3n steps and thus O(n) runtime (iii). Then, we must calculate the starting and ending indices for each major’s section in the line. Given there are two indices to calculate for four categories, we generalize the number of steps to 8. They each occur once during the overall program runtime, so O(1) time complexity from 8 steps during runtime (iv).
Next, we initialize and declare a dictionary valid_pos with a similar structure to groups (four key-value pairs, independent of the line size), which is 1 step throughout the entire program runtime (O(1) time complexity) (v).
Lastly, we iterate through the line n (size) times. In each iteration, there are about 3 steps: checking if the current position in line is valid for a major, assigning someone from that major to that position, and decrementing the number of people representing that major in groups. Those 3 steps occur once per iteration, but n times throughout the entire program because there are n positions to sort people into (because there are n people to make up the line). Since they occur once per iteration, we have 3n steps for overall program runtime, so O(n) time complexity (vi).
Since, for overall program runtime, we have 2 steps before our first loop, 3n steps from the first loop of counting the occurrence of each major, 9 steps before our second loop, and another 3n steps from the second loop for sorting people into position in line, we have exactly 11 + 6n steps for overall program runtime. The 11 steps have a time complexity of O(1), and the 6n steps have a time complexity of O(n) (coefficient 6 becomes trivial as n grows very large, which dominates the time complexity). With O(1) + O(n) program time complexity, O(1) becomes trivial as n grows very large. Since there are no scenarios where the runtime is worse or better than linear time, we have Θ(n) program time complexity.
*Space requirements:* O(1) space complexity.
First, consider the base case: checking whether the line is empty or has one person and ending the function if the line meets the base case requirements. As we perform the conditional check once throughout the entire program, the space complexity required is O(1) to retrieve the value from memory for checking the truth of the case (i). Next, consider the amount of space the groups dictionary requires. If we have four key-value pairs, four being independent of the line’s size, the time complexity is O(1) as the dictionary and its values do not increase in size as the line increases (ii). Now, we count the total occurrence of each major in the line. The conditional for the loop requires O(1) space because the same iterator is assigned a new value on each iteration (iii). Within the loop, each step requires O(1) space (quick conditional check, accessing a value from a dictionary and incrementing by 1, etc) and is deallocated once an iteration finishes, as the variables leave scope (iv). Then, calculating the starting and ending indices for four major sections in the line requires 8 variables, which does not grow as the line grows in size, we have O(1) space complexity (v). Lastly, we iterate through the line n (size) times. In each iteration, the iterator is reassigned a value, so O(1) space complexity for the entire program runtime. Every step within the iteration requires O(1) space complexity and is deallocated once the iteration ends and the variables leave scope (vi).
Since, for overall program runtime, we have 8*O(1) space complexity throughout the entire function runtime, and the function passes the line by reference (the function makes changes to the original array), the overall space complexity of the algorithm is O(1).
### PRACTICALITY
If the algorithm were implemented in the original situation, of students physically lining up for food, and moving into the correct order, the groups dictionary would act similar to a room with four corners and people at each corner. Applying the algorithm, you would go through the line and count each person and their major to account for in groups. The main difference between a “four-corners” approach and the group sort approach is that you are not physically moving each individual person to their respective corner but rather counting them as being in a specific category. I am assuming that if a room can hold a single-file line, the corners’ space can be the line itself or any area surrounding the line. In a real-life situation, the line would not be in a space so tight that people could not have space to move. You could physically mark a position as “.” with a post-it note or sign, but you could also designate each person’s current position as “.” in your brain. By marking the position as “.” no matter the method, you are indicating that each person’s position is a valid position in the line. Then, you can start at the beginning (or front) of the line and pull people from a major to each position. For example, you would say “I need an undeclared major here.” The groups dictionary's empty state would be the sorter physically reaching the end of the line, and it is single-file with no stragglers or remaining people to move/sort in the line.
